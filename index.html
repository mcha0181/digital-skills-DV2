Australia is more connected. But the skills gap persists.

Digital ability is rising nationally, but age and education still make a difference. Explore what the numbers say—and what they do not.

47.4index points separate Australians aged 18–34 and people aged 75 or older in 2025. This is a score gap, not a percentage.
01 / People

Age still shapes who feels at home online.

The Australian Digital Inclusion Index measures digital ability on a 0–100 scale. Older Australians are improving, though they still have a long way to catch up.

The score for people aged 75 and over rose from 23.3 to 41.5 between 2023 and 2025. That is progress, but the gap with younger adults remains large.

02 / Beyond skills

Being connected is not the same as being confident online.

Access, affordability and digital ability are related, but they are not interchangeable. The Census adds another view: how people chose to return their forms.

A note on the two datasetsThe Index measures digital inclusion, while the Census charts measure online form submission. Filling in a Census form online is not, by itself, a test of digital literacy. The 2025 affordability score also uses a changed definition and should not be compared directly with earlier years.
03 / Around Australia

Where the gaps appear on a map.

First, compare states as equal-sized tiles. Then look at online Census participation using circles. Finally, see digital ability across actual state boundaries. The two measures are deliberately kept separate.

The tile map makes comparisons fairer; the choropleth shows geography. Neither map measures an individual person’s skills.

04 / Education and work

For some people, going online is still harder.

The divide is not just about age or postcode. In the 2025 Index, digital ability among employed Australians was 17.6 index points higher than among people outside the labour force. The comparison describes an association, not an effect of employment.

Roughly two-thirds of people aged 75+ were digitally excluded or highly excluded, compared with about one in five Australians overall.

What this can mean in daily lifeStudy materials, job applications and training now often sit behind a screen. These group differences can suggest barriers to online services; they do not show that digital skills caused any individual employment or education outcome.
