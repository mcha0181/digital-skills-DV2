Chapter 01

Skills are improving, but the age gap remains

Digital ability measures the skills and confidence people need to participate in an increasingly digital society. The Australian Digital Inclusion Index highlights substantial differences across age groups and education levels.

Key finding: age remains a major divide

Australians aged 75 and over have made substantial improvements in digital ability. However, their 2025 score remains far below that of younger adults. This suggests that improvements in national averages do not necessarily eliminate digital inequality.

Chapter 02

Where Australians live matters

Digital inclusion varies geographically. Comparing states and territories helps reveal differences in digital ability and opportunities for online participation.

Understanding different indicators

Digital ability, internet access, affordability and online participation describe different aspects of digital inclusion. A higher online Census response rate does not necessarily mean a population has stronger digital skills.

Chapter 03

Three maps, three ways to see the divide

Geographic information can be represented through different visualisation techniques. These maps show how tile positioning, proportional symbols and geographic boundaries communicate state-level differences.

Why use three different maps?

The tile-grid cartogram treats states as equal-sized units, the proportional-symbol map uses circle size to compare scores, and the choropleth colours geographic regions according to their values. Each representation offers a different perspective.

Chapter 04

Why digital skills matter for education and work

Digital capabilities are increasingly relevant to everyday participation in education and employment. Online learning, job applications and workplace communication often depend on digital skills.

Education

Digital skills can support participation in online learning platforms, access to educational resources and communication with teachers or institutions. Differences in digital ability may create additional barriers for learners with fewer digital capabilities.

Employment

Applying for jobs, completing digital training and using workplace technology can all require digital capabilities. People with weaker digital skills may face additional challenges in these activities.

These are potential implications of digital inequality. The charts above do not directly measure employment outcomes or prove that digital ability causes differences in education or employment success.

Data interpretation

Digital ability is represented on a 0–100 index scale. Differences between scores are index points, not percentages.

The 18–34 score of 88.9 and university-education score of 82.7 were reconstructed from published comparisons. The 2025 Index uses survey data collected in 2024.

The 2025 affordability indicator is not directly comparable with earlier years. Census response rates are kept separate from digital ability scores because they measure different concepts.
